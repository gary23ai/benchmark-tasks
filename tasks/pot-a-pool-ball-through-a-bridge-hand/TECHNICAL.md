# Pot the 1-ball: one hand bridges the cue on the cloth, the other strokes it

Two YAM arms stand on a bench at the long rail of a 7-ft home pool table. A house cue lies on the near rail.
The robot has to pick the cue up, make a bridge on the cloth with its left hand so that the shaft slides
through it, hold the cue further back with its right hand, and stroke the white ball into the yellow 1-ball,
cutting it 30 degrees into the corner pocket marked by a chalk cube, without touching any ball except with the
cue's tip on the white.

Both hands act at the same time and both decide the result: the left hand (the bridge) sets the line and
height of the cue's tip, the right hand sets its speed. One hand cannot do it (see "Why the second hand is
needed"), and a strike made without a bridge the shaft slides through scores 0.

Scores (run 4, the final scene, scorer and demonstration): Dream-1 run: 0.0 (1,600 steps; it moved both arms
above the table and never touched the cue or a ball); demo: 1.0 (620 steps). Earlier runs are under
"Simulator runs".

### Cost and set-up, honestly

The bench needs a real pool table at true size: any 7-ft (78 x 39 in playing surface) home slate table with
a corner pocket mouth near 5 in. New ones cost about $1,200 to $3,500, used ones $300 to $1,200 plus moving
and levelling; balls, a 58 in house cue and chalk cost under $100. That is more than most benchmark props, and
it is said plainly here rather than replaced by a miniature: every ball, cushion and pocket in the simulation is
at the table's real size. Cheaper routes to the same bench: a used home table, or a bar or pool-hall 7-ft table
used by the hour (same playing surface; the robot's bench is wheeled up to its long rail, and a ball-return
table needs nothing changed because the scorer only asks which pocket the ball entered). The robot's bench stands against the near long rail with the arms' bases 73 mm
behind the rail's outer edge and level with the station's 760 mm table height; the cloth is 27 mm above the
bench. Reset takes under a minute: re-spot three balls on their marks and lay the cue on the rail at its mark.

## The scene

Tabletop frame: x forward from the line through the two arm bases, y to the robot's left, z up from the
robot's bench (760 mm above the floor, the station's table height). The station's own table is replaced by a
bench just behind the near rail (front edge 8 mm behind the rail's outer edge); the arms, cameras and their
stand are the stock station's.

- Table: near long cushion nose at x = 200, the left short cushion nose at y = 764 (the near-left corner
  pocket is the target), the far rail at x = 1191, the right end at y = -1217; side pockets at y = -226.
  Cloth (bed) at z = 27, rail caps at z = 75. The slate has six real holes; each cushion is a convex prism with
  its nose at 36.3 mm, its ends cut along the pockets' facings; the wooden caps are cut on the same lines;
  under each hole hangs a leather drop pocket.
- Balls: white at (369, 372), yellow 1-ball at (341, 542), red 3-ball at (840, -420) (out of play). The ghost
  ball for the 30-degree cut is 120 mm in front of the white; the 1-ball's path to the pocket's mouth is
  200 mm long and its line passes 56 and 67 mm from the two jaw points (aimed 6 mm toward the near-rail jaw,
  because the far facing converges more on a ball coming in 15 degrees off the pocket's centre line).
- Cue: lying on the near rail's cap (x = 111), tip at y = 400 pointing at the marked pocket, resting on its
  ferrule and its butt.
- A blue chalk cube stands on the near rail beside the marked pocket.

What each policy camera sees at 320 x 176: the overhead camera (the station's) sees the white, the 1-ball, the
marked pocket's mouth and the chalk cube (left edge of the frame), the cue lying on the near rail (its butt
leaves the frame on the right), and both hands at the bridge and the grip; the far rail and the far-left
pocket are just out of view. The wrist cameras look along the fingers (their views were not rendered offline). The review camera (`side_cam`, not a policy input) looks back along the shot from
above the table's left end.

## The bridge

The left gripper points straight down with its fingertips on the cloth and its pads 8 mm apart, closer than
the 13 mm shaft is thick. Read from the YAM's collision hulls (`assets/yam/collision/tip_*`): between the two
fingers, 74.4 mm above the fingertips, the undersides of the two finger carriages form a flat ceiling; at the
shaft's height the fingers' inner faces are 15 mm apart (1 mm of play each side with an 8 mm pad gap); below,
the pads are too close for the shaft to drop through. So the shaft is inside a closed loop formed by the
hand's own fingers: it is the robot's version of a closed bridge, where a player's index finger loops over
the shaft. The cue's axis in the loop is 68 mm above the cloth; with the tip aimed at the white's centre
(28.6 mm up) the cue is raised 7 degrees and the bridge is 290 mm behind the tip, normal values for a player.

Why the shaft presses up into the ceiling: the right hand holds the cue 0.80 m from its tip, 0.20 m in front
of its balance point, so the cue's own weight turns it tip-up with 0.539 x 9.81 x 0.203 = 1.07 N m about the
grip. A pinch resists turning about its pad line hardly at all and about the other axes with roughly 0.1 to
0.3 N m (two 12 to 20 mm wide pads at 25 N), so the rest, about 1.5 to 2 N, presses the shaft against the
loop's ceiling. The bridge thus sets the shaft's height and line, the grip its speed, and the shaft slides
through the loop at each stroke with about 0.5 N of friction (waxed maple on a dusted hand: friction 0.1 on
the shaft against PhysX's default 0.5 on the pads, averaged).

### Why the second hand is needed

Held by one pinch anywhere but its balance point the cue turns: 0.8 N m per 0.15 m of offset, several times the
0.1 to 0.3 N m a pinch resists, so it droops or rises until something stops it. Held exactly at its balance
point it does not droop, but then nothing fixes its attitude: the pinch is free to turn about the line through
its pads and the tip, 1 m away, can wander off the white's centre by tens of millimetres. In the simulator the
control run `POOL_DEMO_VARIANT=one_hand` lets go with the left hand and strokes with the right alone; in the dry
run the cue tips up and misses the white. In every case the scorer gives 0 to a strike without the left hand's
bridge: the shaft must slide at least 40 mm through a left hand that stays put on the cloth. A two-handed shove
(`POOL_DEMO_VARIANT=no_slide`, the left hand pinching the shaft and moving with it) also scores 0.

## Scoring

The scorer (`grading.py`) sees the balls, the cue's pose and both grippers (their fingertip centres from the
joint readings and their openings) every 20 Hz step, and what the contact sensors reported in the 12 physics
steps between. Every rule can be read from a side video, the overhead video and a tape measure.

- Strike: the first physics step in which the cue's tip touches the white.
- Legal strike: at that moment the left fingertips are within 12 mm of the cloth; the cue's axis passes
  between the left fingers (within 12 mm of their midplane), between 13 and 85 mm above their tips, and the
  left pads are closer together than the shaft is thick there; in the 0.5 s before, the left hand moved at most
  15 mm while the cue slid at least 40 mm through it, touching the left hand (the contact sensor between
  the cue and the left gripper fired in at least half of those control steps); the right gripper is closed on the cue (opening at most
  the cue's diameter + 2 mm), its fingertip centre within 20 mm of the axis, and the cue slipped at most 12 mm
  in it.
- Fouls, each a latched 0: a gripper or arm touches any ball; any part of the cue but its tip touches a ball,
  or the tip touches the 1-ball or the 3-ball; the tip touches the white again after it has left it by 5 mm;
  a ball moves over 5 mm before the strike; the white is pocketed or leaves the table; any ball leaves the table.
- Tiers, the highest reached with no foul: 0.1 legal strike; 0.4 the white's first ball contact is the 1-ball;
  0.7 the 1-ball's centre crosses the marked pocket's mouth (the line between its jaw points); 1.0 the 1-ball
  drops into the marked pocket and the white stays on the table. An illegal strike scores 0.
- The trial ends once, after the strike, every ball on the table has been still (under 5 mm/s) for 1 s, or at
  the run limit (1,600 steps, 80 s). Possible scores: 0, 0.1, 0.4, 0.7, 1.0.

## Simulation model (PhysX through Isaac Lab, 240 Hz physics, 20 Hz control)

- Balls are rigid spheres (163 g). PhysX has no rolling resistance: a rolling sphere on a plane never slows.
  The adapter therefore applies, before every physics step, a force at each rolling ball's centre against its
  motion, sized so that a rolling ball slows at 0.098 m/s^2 (rolling resistance 0.01 x g, Dr Dave's middle
  value), and a torque that slows spin about the vertical at 10 rad/s^2; neither ever reverses a motion within
  a step. Sliding friction on the cloth (0.2) is PhysX's own, so a ball hit at its centre slides, then rolls at
  5/7 of its speed, as on a real table.
- The cue is one rigid body of nine convex frustums (tip, ferrule, shaft, taper, joint, forearm, wrap, sleeve,
  bumper), 539 g, its centre of mass at the balance point. Its tip is a leather dome (11 mm radius).
  Restitution combines by the minimum and friction by the average, giving the published contact values
  (table above). PhysX treats impacts slower than its bounce threshold as inelastic; the scene's threshold is
  set to 0.05 m/s (default 0.5) so slow cushion and ball contacts keep their restitution: on the PhysicsScene
  prim's `physxScene:bounceThreshold` after the scene is built, and on the simulation config's
  `bounce_threshold_velocity` field where this Isaac Lab version has one; RESULT.json records what was applied.
- The tip-on-white impulse is resolved by PhysX in one physics step; the adapter logs the force at every
  physics step round the strike, its impulse, and the white's and the cue's speeds (`substep_trace`).
- Nothing in the scene is moved, attached or latched by code; the scripted demonstration reads simulator
  state, as the review rules allow, and moves only the arms.

## Running

Recorded runtime: Isaac Sim 6.1 and Isaac Lab-Arena 0.3.1 (Linux, NVIDIA GPU), Inspect Robots 1.0.0,
inspect-robots-yam 0.38.0 and yam-arena 0.1.0 (the yam-isaac-lab-arena repository), then `pip install -e .`.

```
python -m rdb_pool_bridge.run scripted --out out/scripted     # the scripted demonstration
python -m rdb_pool_bridge.run dream --out out/dream           # one Dream-1 trial (dreamlabs/dream-1-beta)
POOL_DEMO_VARIANT=one_hand python -m rdb_pool_bridge.run scripted --out out/one_hand   # control (dry run: 0)
POOL_DEMO_VARIANT=no_slide python -m rdb_pool_bridge.run scripted --out out/no_slide   # control (dry run: 0)
```

Each trial is seed 0 with zero object jitter, cameras 320 x 176, at most 1,600 actions; Dream-1's action
requests are capped at 128 per trial. `run.py scripted` exits 0 only on a score of 1.0. Wall time in run 4
(one L40S): 159 s for the demonstration's 620 steps, 283 s for Dream-1's 1,600 steps with 33 action requests.

Offline:

```
python -m pytest -q tests
python tools/dryrun_demo.py --yam-arena <yam-isaac-lab-arena checkout> [--clearance <folder with clearance.py>]
python tools/build_assets.py          # regenerate the USD after editing layout.py
```

The dry run (`tools/kinematic_world.py`) has no contact physics: arms follow their commands with a lag and a
sag, the cue follows the hands' pinch points or the loop's ceiling, and the balls are a 2D slide-roll-collide
model. Through the real scorer the demonstration scores 1.0 in it for arm lags of 0.06 to 0.2 s, strikes 25 %
weaker or 30 % stronger, and with or without sag; the worst case tried (0.2 s lag, 15 % weaker, heavy sag) is
a legal strike that hits the 1-ball first and leaves it short (0.4). The two control variants and an idle
robot score 0.

## The scripted demonstration

Closed loop on the cue's measured pose (`scripted.py`): both hands pinch the cue on the rail (the left at 0.29 m
from the tip, top-down; the right at 0.80 m with its fingers leaning 40 degrees); they lift it, slide it along
the rail and lower it onto the shot's line; the left hand opens to 25 mm and drops 61 mm so the shaft rises to
its ceiling, then closes its pads to 8 mm below the shaft; the left hand's x and the right hand are servoed until
the cue's measured axis runs through the white's centre along the shot line; the left fingertips are pressed on
the cloth (command 22 mm below it); the right hand makes a steady backswing of 130 mm, from which the demo fits
how far its arm lags its command, and plans a forward stroke on that lag to meet the white at 0.6 m/s (white
leaves at about 0.8 m/s), running on 80 mm past the contact point. Before the shot it makes one practice
stroke, as players do: the same stroke started 130 mm further back, so it ends about 50 mm short of the white.
At speed the hand swings off the straight line (its joints lag by different amounts); the sideways and vertical
swing measured from the joint readings is fed forward, negated and shifted by the fitted lag, into the real
stroke, up to the step in which the cue should reach the white, and held constant after it. Each arm's
configuration is chosen once for the whole path (closing sign and seed with the most joint margin, at least 7
degrees in the dry run). The dry run reproduces run 2's swing (`swerve`): without the practice stroke that
shot misses; with it the shot scores 1.0 up to 1.6 times run 2's swing at the arm lag measured in Isaac.

## Simulator runs

- Run 1 (scripted): stopped at start-up; this Isaac Lab keeps PhysX settings under `sim.physics`, not
  `sim.physx`. The bounce threshold is now set wherever the config has it and on the USD PhysicsScene prim,
  never failing the trial; run 2 recorded `{"cfg": {"set": ["sim.physics"]}, "usd": {"/physicsScene": 0.05}}`.
- Run 2 (scripted, 563 steps): 0.4. The bridge, the grips and the strike worked as designed: legal strike,
  shaft 64.6 mm above the left fingertips in the loop, cue slid 153 mm through it, bridge moved 5 mm, fitted
  lag 0.075 s, white left at 0.90 m/s (target 0.8 to 0.9), tip impulse 0.145 N s in one physics step, first
  contact the 1-ball. But during the fast forward stroke the right hand swung 16 mm to +x, turning the cue
  1.7 degrees about the bridge; the tip met the white 10 mm left of centre, the white went 10 degrees right of
  the line and only grazed the 1-ball (0.2 m/s instead of about 0.5), which stopped 0.2 m short of the pocket.
  Fixes: the practice strokes above; the shot line is now taken from the measured white and 1-ball; the grip's
  axis tolerance is 20 mm (a pinch holds the TCP 9 mm off the axis by construction; run 2 read 11.8 mm).
- Run 3 (scripted, 1,600 steps): 0.0, no strike. The first practice stroke (uncorrected) measured the hand's
  swing (22 mm worst) and the second, corrected one cut it to 4 mm, but the correction switched on during its
  deceleration jerked the hand 15 mm sideways and down; the cue swung out of the right grip (opening fell to
  12.6 mm, grip force 0) and wedged in the loop, and the real stroke moved an empty hand. That second practice
  stroke, ending only 3 mm short of the white, also came too close. Fixes: one uncorrected practice stroke,
  started 130 mm back; the correction is fed only into the real stroke, stops changing at the contact step, and
  is limited to 30 mm; the practice record logs whether the grip held.
- Run 4 (final): demonstration 1.0 in 620 steps (159 s). The practice stroke kept the grip (worst swing
  21.1 mm, contact step 9, shift 1 step). The strike at step 551 was legal: left fingertips at 27.0 mm (on
  the cloth), shaft 65.4 mm above them in the loop, the cue slid 157 mm through a bridge that moved 6.0 mm and
  touched it in every step of the last 0.5 s; right grip 14.4 mm off the axis, slip 1.5 mm. Tip force 34.7 N
  in one physics step (impulse 0.145 N s); the white left at 0.79 m/s, hit the 1-ball first, and the 1-ball
  dropped into the near-left pocket; the white stopped at (588, 678). The bridge stayed on the cloth and in
  the loop through and after contact (shaft 65.3 to 65.4 mm above the fingertips, left hand on the cue 2 to
  4 N). No fouls; arms never touched each other. Dream-1: 0.0; it waved both arms above the table, closed its
  grippers in the air and never touched the cue or a ball (33 action requests, 1,600 steps).

## Limits

- The layout is fixed (seed 0, no jitter), so a memorised sequence can work; the scripted demonstration reads
  simulator state, as the review rules allow.
- Spin and swerve come only from PhysX friction; the cloth's rolling resistance and spin friction are applied
  as forces (above). Jaw rubber is rigid.
- The cue's profile and balance, pocket depth and hole sizes are estimates; the WPA numbers are read.
- The two control variants (`one_hand`, `no_slide`) were checked in the dry run (both 0), not in Isaac.
